A Brief History of Pidgin English

Pidgin English is a contact language that emerged from trade and colonial encounters, and its history is one of the most fascinating stories of linguistic adaptation in the modern world. This document focuses on the West African variety most relevant to Nigerian students.

1. Where the Word Comes From

The term pidgin itself has a Chinese origin. It began in the early 19th century in Guangzhou, where Chinese merchants interacting with English-speaking traders on the docks adopted and modified the word "business" into pidgin. By the end of that century, the word had become the general descriptor for this type of simplified communication used between people who speak different languages.

2. The Core Origin: Trade and the Atlantic Slave Trade

Linguists agree that Pidgin English developed for a practical purpose: trade. The best-known pidgins emerged in European trade colonies of Africa and the Pacific, around trade forts and along trade routes, before they were later expanded during the era of formal colonisation in the second half of the 19th century.

West African Pidgin English specifically originated as a language of commerce between British and African slave traders during the period of the Atlantic slave trade. It was the lingua franca — the common language of commerce — spoken along the West African coast during that era. The socioeconomic history suggests that these pidgins were based on the nonstandard vernaculars spoken by the European traders, which their African counterparts were exposed to during mercantile encounters.

3. The Nigerian Story: From Portuguese to English

The origins of Nigerian Pidgin are traced to contacts between Europeans and ethnolinguistic groups in the coastal region of Nigeria between 1469 and 1650, first with the Portuguese and the Dutch, then with the British.

The very first European language Nigerians spoke was Portuguese. A Portuguese-based pidgin, known as Negro or Pidgin Portuguese, was the trade language between the indigenous people and Portuguese merchants in the 15th century.

However, the Portuguese pidgin was short-lived. As the Portuguese were ousted by other European traders — the French, the Dutch, and finally the British — an English-based Pidgin developed along the coast from the beginning of the 17th century onward.

A key factor in the rapid spread of Nigerian Pidgin was Nigeria's extreme linguistic diversity. With hundreds of indigenous languages and no single ethnic group able to dominate the others, Pidgin became the neutral common tongue, especially in the linguistically heterogeneous southern regions.

4. From Pidgin to Creole: A Language Comes of Age

A crucial linguistic distinction:

  • A pidgin is a simplified contact language with no native speakers.
  • A creole is a fully developed language with native speakers.

Nigerian Pidgin has crossed that threshold. Linguists now classify Nigerian and Cameroonian "Pidgin" English as creolisations of an initial pidgin — languages born of an ancestral trade pidgin that have evolved into full communal systems.

Today, West African Pidgin English is spoken by an estimated 75 million people across Nigeria, Cameroon, Ghana, and Equatorial Guinea. Nigerian Pidgin alone has roughly 4.7 million first-language speakers and 116 million second-language speakers as of 2020.

It coexists with standard Nigerian English: the pidgin serves as an indigenous vernacular and lingua franca, while the standard variety is associated with the intellectual elite.

5. Recognition and Stigma

For much of its history, Pidgin was dismissed as "broken English". But that perception is changing. The launch of BBC Pidgin on the World Service in 2017 was a landmark moment — recognition that this is not "broken" but a beautiful, fully functional language in its own right.

It is now used in literature, political campaigns, and broadcast media across West Africa.

Pidgin English began as a rough trade language on the West African coast in the 15th–17th centuries, evolved from Portuguese to English as colonial powers shifted, and has since grown into a creole spoken by hundreds of millions — a living testament to linguistic creativity born of necessity.
