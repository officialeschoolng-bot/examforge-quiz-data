BIO 101 — Evolution & Darwinism
Natural selection, Lamarckism, and the genetics of inheritance

Darwin's Observations

Darwin's Observations
Variation Among Individuals: Individuals within a species are not identical. Traits vary in size, color, shape, and behavior. These variations are often heritable.
Struggle for Existence: Populations grow faster than resources. This leads to constant competition for food, space, and mates.
Natural Selection: Individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce. Less advantageous traits are gradually eliminated.
Descent with Modification: Species evolve over time as small changes accumulate across generations. Over long periods, this leads to the emergence of new species (speciation).
Common Ancestry: All living organisms share a common ancestry. Closely related species have a more recent common ancestor.
KEY POINT
Darwin's core logic: heritable variation + struggle for existence = natural selection → descent with modification → speciation.
Publication and Modern Context
Darwin published his ideas in On the Origin of Species (1859).
The modern synthesis in the 1930s and 1940s integrated Darwin's natural selection with Mendelian genetics.
Key Concepts in the Modern Synthesis
  1. Genetic Variation: Driven by mutations, genetic recombination, and gene flow.
  2. Population Genetics: Evolution occurs within populations, changing allele distributions over time.
  3. Speciation: New species form when populations become isolated and diverge genetically.
  4. Punctuated Equilibrium: Proposed by Stephen Jay Gould; evolution occurs in rapid bursts followed by long periods of stability.
Criticisms and Misconceptions
Social Darwinism: The controversial misapplication of natural selection to human societies.
Religious Opposition: Groups opposing the idea of common ancestry, leading to creationism and intelligent design.
Lamarckism: The discredited idea that species evolve through the inheritance of acquired traits.

Lamarckism

Lamarckism
Proposed by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829) in Philosophie Zoologique (1809).
Key Principles:
  1. Use and Disuse of Organs: Frequently used organs become stronger and larger; unused ones weaken and disappear.
  2. Inheritance of Acquired Characteristics: Traits acquired during an organism's lifetime are passed to offspring.
Criticism of Lamarckism
  1. There is no evidence that acquired traits can be inherited.
  2. Mendelian genetics proved that inheritance is based on discrete units (genes).
  3. Darwin's natural selection provided a more plausible mechanism.
  4. Lamarck provided no clear mechanism for how acquired traits are transmitted to offspring.
KEY POINT
Lamarck: acquired traits are inherited. Darwin: heritable variation is selected. Mendelian genetics supports Darwin, not Lamarck.

Key Genetic Concepts

Genetic Terminology
Term Meaning
DNA Double helix molecule containing genetic instructions. Bases: Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Cytosine (C), Guanine (G).
Gene Basic unit of heredity. A segment of DNA containing instructions to produce proteins.
Chromosomes Thread-like structures of DNA and protein in the nucleus. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
Alleles Different versions of a gene at a specific locus. Can be dominant or recessive.
Genotype & Phenotype Genotype is the genetic makeup. Phenotype is the observable physical or physiological traits.
Homozygous & Heterozygous Homozygous = two identical alleles. Heterozygous = two different alleles.
Dominance & Recessiveness A dominant allele masks the expression of a recessive allele.
Genetic Variation
Differences in DNA sequences within a population.
Arises through mutations, recombination during meiosis, gene flow, and genetic drift.
Meiosis and Genetic Recombination
Meiosis produces gametes with half the number of chromosomes.
Homologous chromosomes exchange genetic material through crossing over, creating genetic diversity.
Mutations
Changes in the DNA sequence.
Types include silent mutations (no effect), missense mutations (changes one amino acid), nonsense mutations (premature stop codon), and frameshift mutations (changes reading frame).
Epigenetics
Changes in gene expression without altering the DNA sequence.
Includes DNA methylation and histone modification.
Genetic Engineering
Direct manipulation of an organism's genome using biotechnology, such as CRISPR-Cas9.

Mendelian Laws & Modern Genetics

Mendelian Laws of Inheritance
Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) experimented with pea plants.
  1. Law of Segregation: Two alleles for each trait separate during gamete formation.
  2. Law of Independent Assortment: Alleles for different genes are passed to offspring independently (e.g., 9:3:3:1 ratio).
  3. Law of Dominance: In a heterozygous condition, the dominant allele is expressed while the recessive allele is masked.
Modern Genetics
Polygenic inheritance: Multiple genes controlling a trait (e.g., height, skin color).
Gene linkage: Genes close together on the same chromosome are inherited together.
Epistasis: One gene masks or modifies the expression of another gene.
Incomplete Dominance: The heterozygote expresses an intermediate phenotype (e.g., pink snapdragons).
Codominance: Both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote (e.g., AB blood type).
Multiple Alleles: More than two alleles exist for a gene in a population (e.g., A, B, and O blood types).
Pleiotropy: A single gene influences multiple phenotypic traits.
