Origin of Spectrum

Origin of Spectrum
The observation of hydrogen spectra explained the dual nature of the electron, showing that it behaves as both a wave and a particle.
The wave motion of electrons is characterized by three quantities: wave velocity (c), wavelength (λ), and frequency (ν).
The velocity of light, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
Wavelength is measured in millimicron (mµ) or angstrom (Å), where 1 Å = 10⁻⁸ cm.
A spectrum is produced by the dispersion of a beam of light by a prism to obtain different frequencies.
c = λ × ν
Frequency is the reciprocal of wavelength (ν = 1/λ) and is measured in cm⁻¹.
When substances are heated to a high temperature, they emit radiations which can be resolved into different wavelengths by a spectrograph.
When an electric discharge (high voltage) is passed through gaseous atoms enclosed in a tube at low pressure, the gaseous atoms are excited and emit radiation.

Types of Spectra
As electrons are emitted from hot surfaces, they have wave motion.
If solid substances are heated, they produce continuous line spectra.
Gaseous substances give sharp line spectra or band spectra — shown as discrete, separated parallel lines.
KEY POINT
Solids → continuous spectrum. Gases → line (or band) spectrum.

3.1 Hydrogen Spectrum

Hydrogen Atom & Balmer's Contribution
The hydrogen atom (protium) has one electron, one proton, and an atomic radius of 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm.
When hydrogen gas at reduced pressure is excited by passing an electric discharge, the molecules are dissociated and the atoms emit light at a series of frequencies, forming a spectrum with sharp lines.
Balmer pointed out that the wavelength of light in the visible region fitted the expression:
1/λ = R × (1/2² − 1/n²)
where n = 3, 4, 5, … and R is the Rydberg constant with a value of 1.09679 × 10⁵ cm⁻¹.
Rydberg contributed to the formation of the general formula:
1/λ = V̄ = R × (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)
where V̄ is the wave-number, λ is the wavelength, and n₁ and n₂ are the lower and higher energy levels respectively.
Hydrogen Series
Series Region Equation n₂ Values
Lyman UV V̄ = R(1/1² − 1/n₂²) 2, 3, 4, …
Balmer Visible V̄ = R(1/2² − 1/n₂²) 3, 4, 5, …
Paschen IR V̄ = R(1/3² − 1/n₂²) 4, 5, 6, …
Brackett IR V̄ = R(1/4² − 1/n₂²) 5, 6, 7, …
Pfund IR V̄ = R(1/5² − 1/n₂²) 6, 7, 8, …
Ritz Equation
1/λ = V̄ = R × (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)
c = wave velocity, λ = wavelength, ν = frequency, R = Rydberg constant, n₁ = lower energy level, n₂ = higher energy level.
WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — BALMER → BRACKETT
Question: What is the wave number and wavelength of an electron excited from the Balmer series to the Brackett series (R = 109737 cm⁻¹)?
Step 1 — Apply Ritz equation:
V̄ = 109737 × (1/2² − 1/4²)
V̄ = 109737 × (1/4 − 1/16) = 109737 × (3/16)
V̄ = 2.06 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹

Step 2 — Calculate wavelength:
λ = 1 / V̄ = 1 / (2.06 × 10⁴) = 4.86 × 10⁻⁵ cm
Lyman Series Calculation
ADDITIONAL PRACTICE — FIRST LYMAN LINE
Question: Calculate the wave number and wavelength of the first line in the Lyman series (R = 1.097 × 10⁵ cm⁻¹). Transition: n₂ = 2 → n₁ = 1.
Step 1 — Ritz equation:
V̄ = 1.097 × 10⁵ × (1/1² − 1/2²)
V̄ = 1.097 × 10⁵ × (1 − 1/4) = 1.097 × 10⁵ × (3/4)
V̄ = 8.2275 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹

Step 2 — Wavelength:
λ = 1 / V̄ = 1 / (8.2275 × 10⁴) = 1.215 × 10⁻⁵ cm

3.2 Gravimetric Laws

Introduction
Gravimetry is an analytical method that involves weight measurement as a way of determining the constituents of any substance.
There are two basic gravimetric laws: the law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportion.
3.2.1 Law of Conservation of Mass
Lavoisier's experiment showed that any combustion process involves the reaction of the substance with oxygen.
If a reaction is carried out in an enclosed system, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products at completion.
Matter can neither be created nor destroyed but can be transformed from one form to another.
EXAMPLE REACTION
BaCl₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2HCl
Total weight of reactants = total weight of products (in an enclosed system).
3.2.2 Law of Definite Proportion
This law states that all pure samples of a particular chemical compound contain similar elements combined in the same proportion by mass.
A pure substance such as water would always have the same ratio of hydrogen to oxygen irrespective of the method of preparation or where it was obtained.
3.2.3 Inexactness of the Law of Conservation of Mass
In some chemical reactions, energy can be absorbed or evolved, leading to mass destruction or creation.
When energy is evolved, mass is destroyed (e.g., radioactivity). When energy is absorbed, mass is created.
The mass created or destroyed is calculated using Einstein's relativity equation:
E = Δm × c²
E = energy liberated or absorbed, Δm = mass created or destroyed, c = velocity of electromagnetic radiation.
3.2.4 Inexactness in the Law of Definite Proportion
This law is not exactly correct.
It has been invalidated by some metals with variable oxidation states and the introduction into a crystal of higher valence ions in place of lower valence ions.
Examples include Cu₂O and CuO, as well as FeS, Fe₃S₂, Fe₂O₃, and FeO.
Gravimetric Calculation
WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — CaCO₃ DECOMPOSITION
Question: 10 g of CaCO₃ was heated to give 5.6 g of CaO and some CO₂. If CO₂ has a density of 1.97 g/L, calculate the volume of CO₂ liberated if mass is conserved. (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16).
Step 1 — Conservation of mass:
Mass of CaCO₃ = Mass of CaO + Mass of CO₂
10 g = 5.6 g + Mass of CO₂
Mass of CO₂ = 10 − 5.6 = 4.4 g

Step 2 — Volume using density:
Volume = Mass / Density = 4.4 / 1.97
Volume = 2.23 L
Verifying Conservation of Mass
ADDITIONAL PRACTICE — MCO₃ DECOMPOSITION
Question: 5 g of metal carbonate (MCO₃) is heated and leaves 2.8 g of metal oxide (MO). If molar mass of metal is 40 g/mol, verify the law of conservation of mass.
Equation: MCO₃ → MO + CO₂

Conservation of mass:
5 g = 2.8 g + Mass of CO₂ ⟹ Mass of CO₂ = 2.2 g

Stoichiometric verification:
Molar mass MCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 g/mol
Moles MCO₃ = 5 / 100 = 0.05 mol
Moles CO₂ = 0.05 mol (1:1 ratio)
Molar mass CO₂ = 44 g/mol
Theoretical mass CO₂ = 0.05 × 44 = 2.2 g

Matches — law verified.

Key Takeaways

Summary
  1. The wave motion of electrons is defined by wave velocity, wavelength, and frequency, related by c = λ × ν.
  2. The hydrogen spectrum consists of distinct series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund) corresponding to electron transitions between energy levels.
  3. The Ritz equation, 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), is used to calculate the wave number of any spectral line in hydrogen.
  4. The law of conservation of mass: matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction — mass of reactants equals mass of products.
  5. The law of definite proportion: a pure compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass.
  6. The mass defect in nuclear reactions and the variable oxidation states of metals are exceptions to classical gravimetric laws.
  7. Hydrogen spectrum calculations require the Rydberg constant (R = 1.097 × 10⁵ cm⁻¹).
  8. Gravimetric calculations use conservation of mass to find missing masses, then apply density or stoichiometric relationships.
