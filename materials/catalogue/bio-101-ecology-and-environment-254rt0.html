BIO 101 — Ecology & Environment
Ecological organization, habitats, niches, and energy flow

Definition & Scope

Definition of Ecology
Ecology is the scientific study of relationships between living organisms and their environment.
The term was coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866 from the Greek words oikos (house) and logos (study).
It covers interactions among organisms and with physical and chemical factors.
Ecology extends to populations, communities, ecosystems, and the biosphere.
KEY POINT
Ecology = study of interactions between organisms and their environment (oikos = house, logos = study).
Levels of Ecological Organization
  1. Organism: Fundamental unit of ecology with independent life.
  2. Population: Group of organisms of the same species in a defined area.
  3. Community: Different species living and interacting in an area.
  4. Ecosystem: Biological community plus all abiotic factors.
  5. Biome: Distinct ecological community in a particular climate (e.g., Sahara desert).
  6. Biosphere: Sum of all ecosystems; the part of Earth where life exists.
Types of Ecology
  1. Autecology: Studies individual organisms and their adaptations.
  2. Synecology: Studies communities, composition, and diversity.
  3. Population Ecology: Studies population size, density, and dynamics.
  4. Community Ecology: Studies interspecific interactions like competition and succession.
  5. Ecosystem Ecology: Analyzes energy flow and nutrient cycling.
  6. Global Ecology: Studies the biosphere, climate change, and human impacts.

Habitats & Niches

Types of Animal Habitats
  1. Terrestrial: Forests, grasslands, deserts, and tundra.
  2. Aquatic: Freshwater (rivers, lakes) and marine (oceans, estuaries).
  3. Arboreal: Located in tree canopies and branches.
  4. Underground: Burrows and tunnels (e.g., earthworms, termites).
Types of Plant Habitats
  1. Terrestrial: Land environments where plants have well-developed roots.
  2. Aquatic: Both freshwater and marine environments.
  3. Epiphytic: Grow on other plants for support, not nutrients (e.g., orchids).
  4. Parasitic: Obtain nutrients from host plants via haustoria (e.g., mistletoe).
Niche
  1. A niche is the way an organism lives in its habitat.
  2. It includes feeding habits, reproductive strategies, and environmental tolerance.
  3. No two species can occupy exactly the same niche long-term due to competitive exclusion.
  4. Species often partition resources to reduce competition.
KEY POINT
Habitat = the address; Niche = the occupation. Competitive exclusion prevents two species from sharing an identical niche.

Biotic & Abiotic Components

Biotic Components
  1. Producers: Make their own food via photosynthesis (e.g., green plants).
  2. Consumers: Depend on producers; include herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores.
  3. Decomposers: Break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil.
Abiotic Components
  1. These are non-living physical and chemical factors.
  2. They include light, temperature, water, soil, air, and humidity.
  3. They shape plant growth, animal behavior, and species distribution.
Relationship Between Components
  1. Plants depend on sunlight, water, and soil nutrients.
  2. Animals depend on plants or other animals for food.
  3. Decomposers release minerals back into the soil for plant use.

Food Chains, Food Webs & Evolution

Food Chain and Food Web
A food chain is a single, direct pathway of energy transfer.
Trophic levels include primary producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and tertiary consumers.
A food web consists of multiple interconnected food chains, reflecting realistic energy flow.
Food webs show how ecosystems remain stable through alternative food sources.
KEY POINT
Trophic order: Producer → Primary consumer → Secondary consumer → Tertiary consumer.
Ecology and Evolution
  1. Ecology determines which traits are useful in a habitat.
  2. Evolution shapes interactions and adaptations over time.
  3. Co-evolution is seen in predator-prey and pollinator-plant relationships.
