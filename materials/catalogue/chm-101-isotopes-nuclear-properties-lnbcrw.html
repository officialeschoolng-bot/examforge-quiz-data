INORGANIC CHEMISTRY — Isotopes & Nuclear Properties
Definitions, RAM calculations, stability, and nuclear relationships

Definition of Isotopes

Isotopes
Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different neutron numbers.
They possess identical chemical properties but differ in physical properties.
J.J. Thomson (1913) first discovered isotopes using neon, showing atoms of mass 20 and 22.
Hydrogen exhibits three isotopes: Protium (¹H), Deuterium (²H), and Tritium (³H).
The mass number of an isotope is written as a superscript before the element symbol.
KEY POINT
Isotopes: same atomic number (protons), different mass number (neutrons). Chemical properties identical; physical properties differ.

Atomic Weight Scale

Atomic Weight Scale
The modern standard for atomic weight is based on the carbon-12 isotope (¹²C = 12.000).
Nineteen elements are composed of only one type of atom and do not exhibit isotopy.
The relative atomic mass (RAM) is calculated from the relative abundance of isotopes.

Calculations of Relative Atomic Mass (RAM)

RAM Formula
RAM = Σ (% abundance × mass number)100
WORKED EXAMPLE — CARBON
Given: ¹²C is 98.9% abundant, ¹³C is 1.1% abundant. Calculate RAM.
RAM = [(1.1 × 13) + (98.9 × 12)] / 100
= (14.3 + 1186.8) / 100
= 1201.1 / 100 = 12.01
Chlorine Ratio Calculation
WORKED EXAMPLE — RATIO
Given: ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl exist in a ratio of 3:1. Find the RAM.
Total atoms = 3 + 1 = 4
RAM = [(3 × 35) + (1 × 37)] / 4
= (105 + 37) / 4
= 142 / 4 = 35.5
Chlorine Abundance Calculation
WORKED EXAMPLE — ABUNDANCE
Given: Sample RAM = 35.5. Find the percentage of ³⁷Cl.
Let X = % of ³⁷Cl, then (100 − X) = % of ³⁵Cl.
[37X + 35(100 − X)] / 100 = 35.5
37X + 3500 − 35X = 3550
2X = 50
X = 25% of ³⁷Cl

Stability of Isotopes

Stability Rules
  1. Light atoms are stable when the neutron to proton ratio is approximately 1.
  2. Nuclei with more than 82 protons are unstable.
  3. Magic numbers: Nuclei with 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, or 126 protons or neutrons are exceptionally stable.
  4. Isotopes with even numbers of protons or neutrons are more stable than those with odd numbers.
Decay Modes
Decay Emitted Particle Effect on Nucleus
Alpha (α) Alpha particle Mass −4, atomic number −2
Beta (β) Beta particle Mass unchanged, atomic number +1

Detection & Separation of Isotopes

Methods
1. Mass spectrograph: Developed by Aston (1913) to identify isotopes.
2. Electromagnetic separation: Used for large-scale separation.
3. Thermal diffusion: Lighter molecules diffuse to hotter parts of a temperature gradient.
4. Fractional distillation: Separates isotopes based on boiling point differences (H₂O vs D₂O).
5. Electrolysis: H₂O dissociates faster than D₂O during electrolysis.
6. Chemical exchange reaction: Temperature-dependent reversible reactions.
7. Centrifugation: High-speed rotation forces heavier isotopes to settle at the edge.

Ions & Isoelectronic Species

Ions
Anions are negatively charged ions formed by gaining electrons.
Cations are positively charged ions formed by losing electrons.
Fajans's Rule: Ions form easily if the electron structure is stable, the charge is small, the cation is large, and the anion is small.
KEY POINT
Isoelectronic species have the same number of electrons (e.g., O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ — all 10 electrons).

Nuclear Relationships

Isotopes, Isobars & Isotones
Relationship Definition Example
Isotopes Same atomic number, different mass number. ¹H, ²H, ³H
Isobars Different elements with the same nucleon number. ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K, ⁴⁰Ca
Isotones Same number of neutrons, different nucleon number. ³⁰Si, ³¹P, ³²S

Mass Defect & Binding Energy

Mass Defect, Binding Energy & Packing Fraction
Mass defect is the difference between the actual measured mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its constituent nucleons.
Binding energy is the energy equivalent of the mass defect that holds the nucleons together.
Binding energy per nucleon measures the magnitude of the force binding the nucleons and indicates nuclear stability.
Packing fraction (PA) is the relative difference between the actual nuclear mass and the mass number.
PA = Actual mass − Mass numberMass number
WORKED EXAMPLE — IRON-56
Given: Iron-56 (Mass number A = 56), observed mass M = 55.847. Calculate the packing fraction.
PA = (55.847 − 56) / 56
= −0.153 / 56
= −2.73 × 10⁻²
Packing fraction has no unit.
