BIO 101 — Interrelationship of Organisms
Competition, predation, parasitism, symbiosis & saprophytism

Overview

Overview of Interrelationships
No organism lives in isolation; they interact with one another.
Intraspecific relationships occur between organisms of the same species.
Interspecific relationships occur between organisms of different species.
Interactions can be direct (physical contact) or indirect (shared resources like food or territory).
Outcomes can be beneficial (+), harmful (−), or neutral (0) to the organisms involved.
KEY POINT
Sign notation reads as (effect on organism A, effect on organism B): + = benefit, − = harm, 0 = neutral.

Competition

Competition
Competition is an interaction where organisms vie for the same scarce resources (food, water, shelter, light).
It prevents organisms from fully occupying their niche.
Types & Forms of Competition
Types:
  1. Intraspecific: Among individuals of the same species.
  2. Interspecific: Among individuals of different species.
Forms:
  1. Exploitative (Scramble): Organisms consume a shared, limited resource.
  2. Interference (Contest): Organisms directly impede each other via aggressive acts or shading.
Significance of Competition
  1. Ensures survival of the fittest.
  2. Regulates population sizes.
  3. Drives evolution of new traits.
  4. Maintains ecosystem balance by preventing dominance by one organism.

Predation

Predation
One organism (predator) benefits by killing and consuming another (prey).
The predator gains nourishment while the prey suffers.
Predators are usually larger and stronger than their prey.
EXAMPLES
1. Lion and zebra
2. Hawk and chick
3. Cat and rat
4. Wolves and rabbits
5. Frogs and grasshoppers

Parasitism

Parasitism
Parasitism is a close association where one organism (parasite) benefits at the expense of another (host).
The parasite causes harm but usually does not kill the host immediately.
Hosts are generally larger than parasites.
Parasitoids differ from parasites; they are insects that lay eggs inside a host, and the larvae consume the host.
Mosquitoes are not true parasites because their association with the host is too brief.
Plant parasitism includes mistletoe inserting roots into trees to extract nutrients, while witchweed and dodder are other examples.
KEY POINT
Parasite = lives with the host long-term. Parasitoid = invariably kills the host. Mosquito = brief feeder, not a true parasite.
Types of Parasites
  1. Ectoparasites: Live on the body of the host (e.g., ticks, fleas, lice, mites).
  2. Endoparasites: Live inside the host's body, highly adapted (e.g., roundworm, hookworm, tapeworm).
  3. Brood Parasites: Do not attack the body; they trick others into raising their young (e.g., European cuckoos, cowbirds).

Symbiosis

Symbiosis
Symbiosis is a long-term, permanent relationship between two different organisms.
Both organisms serve as a support system for each other.
The three categories of symbiosis are Commensalism, Mutualism, and Parasitism.
Examples of Symbiotic Associations
Lichens: Algae/cyanobacteria provide food via photosynthesis; fungi provide shelter and absorb water.
Mycorrhizae: Fungi aid plant nutrient absorption; plant roots provide carbohydrates to fungi.
Rhizobium bacteria: Live in root nodules of legumes, fixing atmospheric nitrogen for the plant.
Clownfish and Sea Anemone: Anemone provides protection with stinging cells; clownfish provide nutrients via food scraps.
Mutualism (+, +)
Mutualism is a binding relationship where both organisms benefit.
Examples:
  1. Pollinating insects and flowers: Insects get nectar; flowers get pollinated.
  2. Humans and gut bacteria: Bacteria help digestion; humans provide a favorable environment and food.
  3. Bacteria and protozoa in herbivore rumen: Microbes digest cellulose; herbivore provides food and shelter.
Commensalism (+, 0)
Commensalism is a relationship where one organism benefits (+), while the other is neither helped nor harmed (0).
Examples:
  1. Remora fish and shark: Remora eats shark's scraps and gets protection; shark is unaffected.
  2. Spider web on a tree: Spider benefits; tree is unaffected.
  3. Bacteria in the human large intestine: Feed on unwanted food; human is unaffected.
  4. Cattle egrets and grazing animals: Egrets eat insects disturbed by hooves; grazing animals are unaffected.
  5. Oyster and crab: Crab lives in oyster shell for protection; oyster is unharmed.
  6. Tree frogs and trees: Frog uses the tree for habitat; tree is unaffected.

Saprophytism

Saprophytism (+, 0)
Saprophytism is a relationship where one organism (saprophyte) obtains nutrients from dead or decaying organic matter.
The dead organism does not benefit.
No direct competition, predation, or mutualism is involved.
Ecological Roles
  1. Decompose organic matter.
  2. Recycle nutrients, making them available to other organisms.
  3. Maintain ecosystem balance via nutrient cycling.
EXAMPLES
1. Fungi decomposing gemlina wood.
2. Mushrooms decomposing soil matter.
3. Bacteria breaking down dead animal matter.
